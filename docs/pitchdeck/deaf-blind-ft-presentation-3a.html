Meet Wally & Dave

Meet Wally and Dave.

The hilarious Richard Pryor plays Wally. Wally is competely blind. Yet he pretends that he can see. So he has built up a system around him of memorised cues and contextual guesses. He reads newspapers he cannot see. Navigates the world on muscle memory and sheer guile. Which is not the same thing as being able to see. [pause]

Gene Wilder plays Wally’s partner Dave. Dave is deaf. Like Wally, he has become very good at pretending that he can hear. He lip-reads. He guesses. He fills the silence with whatever his training gives him — and delivers the result with complete conviction. [pause]

Together, they are unstoppable. Wally strides forward. Dave shouts what he thinks he lip-read. Each one borrows the faculty the other is performing. Neither of them actually has it. What makes it funny is that everyone around them can see exactly what’s happening. Everyone except them. [click]

Silicon Valley has spent a decade equipping AI with eyes.

The visual encoder is Wally — performing sight, converting what it sees into the expected answer. The language model is Dave — filling the gaps with linguistic priors, delivering direction based on what it thinks it understood.

Wally and Dave. Running simultaneously. In every multimodal AI system we are currently using.

Our input is real. The confidence of the model is real. The sight and hearing are not.

Spatial Truth
Collapses

The perception gap is also encountered when designing for blind and low-vision users. They have found that AI-mediated visual descriptions collapse on the information those users need most. The spatial structure around them.

The models frequently fail to genuinely parse space. They cannot reconstruct it for people whose relationship to space is already precarious.

Asking Wally for directions has consequences in real life. His performance of sight can cause real harm.

This is not an
AI problem.
It is a human
problem.

This gap in perception seems to be a human failure, not a technical one. We blindly feed the AI models expecting miraculous responses. We first need to bring the spatial reality and lived experience into the room. The AI cannot know what it hasn’t been given. It confidently performs based on learned patterns like Wally. We’ve confused well-structured prompts with insight, creating solutions that are technically coherent but humanly wrong. We have handed our eyes to something blind.

So next time you see an AI demo that looks like magic, just remember what’s actually happening underneath. In most cases, it’s not seeing the world. It’s receiving a description of the world. and talking confidently from that.

It’s Dave shouting directions. And Wally striding forward like he owns the place. [smiling]
