Methodology

How strategic choice, organisational learning and human judgement come together in practical executive workshops.

The Spotted Ape

How the work fits together

Strategy identifies the choices an organisation must make. Learning develops the capability to act on them. Judgement tests whether the choices and their consequences remain sound.

01 / Strategy as choice

A strategy connects choices about where to compete and how to create an advantage.

In the approach described by A.G. Lafley and Roger Martin in Playing to Win, strategy connects choices about ambition, competitive focus, advantage, capabilities and supporting management systems. Their Strategy Choice Cascade helps teams examine whether these choices support one another.

The Strategy Choice Cascade matters because its choices constrain and reinforce one another. A new ambition without a choice of arena is difficult to act on. An arena without a distinctive way to win can become a market description. A proposed advantage without the capability and operating conditions to sustain it remains an assertion.

We help teams make these links visible, including the choices they will not pursue. Analysis informs the choices; it does not remove the responsibility to make them.

02 / Design and inquiry

Use evidence to make uncertainty workable.

Strategic questions rarely arrive as problems with a single known answer. Design thinking helps teams move between understanding a situation, framing a consequential problem, generating alternatives and learning from tests. It keeps attention on the people, systems and conditions that shape whether a choice can work.

Deductive reasoning asks what follows from stated premises. Inductive reasoning looks for patterns in observations. Abductive reasoning asks what might explain what is happening, or what could plausibly work. It creates hypotheses, not proof. For a proposed strategy, the team identifies the conditions it requires and the evidence that would challenge those conditions. This strategic inquiry draws on Roger Martin’s question, “What would have to be true?”.

The practical aim is not endless ideation. It is to turn uncertain assumptions into questions that can change a decision.

03 / Organisational learning

Knowledge must travel into practice.

Documents, procedures and repositories preserve knowledge. They are useful stocks, but a stock is not the same as the ability to apply, adapt or question what is stored. Some important knowledge is tacit: people develop it through participation, observation, adjustment and work with others.

Learning therefore depends on flows as well as storage. Who can reach whom? Where can a practitioner show how a decision is made? What recurring work allows another person to try, receive feedback and improve? What happens when an experiment reveals that an established assumption no longer fits?

Communities of practice and creation spaces can make such exchange possible. They need a real problem, access to relevant people and resources, room to make and test, and a path back to decisions and operating practice. They are not simply new names for training rooms or repositories.

04 / Knowing, making, playing

A different way to learn can reveal a different problem.

John Seely Brown’s account of learning makes room for three complementary modes. Knowing brings concepts, evidence and experience into view. Making gives an idea a form that can be examined. Playing makes it possible to try a variation, change perspective and reframe the question.

In a strategy setting, a team might first describe what it believes about customers and competition; sketch a different way to create value; then use a scenario to test how a customer, competitor or colleague might respond. The sketch is not a forecast. It is a concrete hypothesis the team can discuss, revise or set aside.

This sequence can open a new problem space without treating play as an end in itself. Each activity should inform a strategic question or a capability the organisation needs to develop.

05 / Designed executive learning

The learning design is part of the intervention.

Before selecting activities, we need to understand who is in the room, what they already know, which decisions they can influence and what they need to do afterwards. Learning outcomes should be specific enough to guide both the session and its tangible outputs.

We distinguish three kinds of outcome:

Knowledge

What participants need to understand: a strategic concept, the evidence available, or the structure of a decision.

Skills

What participants need to practise: mapping choices, articulating assumptions, prototyping a scenario or tracing knowledge through work.

Wisdom

What participants need to judge: which evidence is sufficient, what uncertainty to accept, what to test next and who remains accountable.

The format follows the outcome. A short explanation can introduce a concept; participants then apply it to a live or representative problem. A scenario can ask them to judge a choice from another stakeholder’s position. Discussion and critique make reasoning visible. Breaks and changes of activity are planned around the audience and the work, not a claimed universal minute-by-minute rule.

Participants should leave with a useful working artefact: for example, a record of strategic choices structured using the Strategy Choice Cascade, a hypothesis-and-test map, a knowledge-flow blueprint, a scenario prototype or explicit decision rights. It is a basis for further work, not a guarantee that an organisation will implement it.

06 / From choice to action

One connected working sequence.

  1. Frame the decisionAgree the organisational question, participants, evidence and boundaries.
  2. Make choices explicitUse the Strategy Choice Cascade to connect ambition, competitive focus, advantage, capabilities and supporting management practices.
  3. Surface assumptionsIdentify the conditions each option requires and the evidence that would change the team’s view.
  4. Make and testUse a scenario, prototype or work-based activity to examine a hypothesis.
  5. Design for learningIdentify the knowledge, practice and relationships needed to develop capability.
  6. Decide and reviewRecord the choice, unresolved questions, owners and a proportionate next test.

The sequence is adapted to the work. A team may return to an earlier choice when new evidence changes the problem; the sequence is a guide for inquiry, not a maturity scale.

Judgement and responsibility

Tools can contribute. People still own consequential choices.

AI can alter how quickly teams generate options, analyse information and produce work. That makes the purpose, evidence threshold, decision rights and learning consequences more important to specify. Where a system recommends or acts, a useful design asks who can inspect it, challenge it, stop it and remain accountable for the outcome.

This concern connects the strategic and learning work to The Spotted Ape’s existing interest in human judgement, agency and organisational integrity. It is relevant where AI is changing work, but it does not require every strategy or learning problem to be framed as an AI problem.

Our strategy work draws on the Strategy Choice Cascade described in Playing to Win by A.G. Lafley and Roger Martin. Our learning design also draws on John Seely Brown and Fitzpatrick & Hunt. Our contribution is to combine established strategic approaches with behavioural design, practical learning and human judgement, applying them to the organisation’s decisions and working conditions. These are intellectual foundations, not partnerships, endorsements or proprietary frameworks of The Spotted Ape.
